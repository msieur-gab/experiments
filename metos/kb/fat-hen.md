title: Fat hen
aka: lamb's quarters, white goosefoot, wild spinach, pigweed, Weißer Gänsefuß, Melde, chénopode blanc, ansérine, Chenopodium album

warn: It contains oxalic acid and can hold a lot of nitrate on heavily fertilised ground. Eat it cooked and in moderate amounts. Do not confuse it with black nightshade or thorn apple, which are poisonous and grow in the same places.

A common weed of gardens, fields and disturbed ground. It is closely related to quinoa and spinach and has been eaten as a vegetable in Europe for thousands of years. Leaves and seeds are both food.

## identify
- Young tips and leaf undersides are covered in a white, mealy powder, as if dusted with flour. Water beads off them.
- Leaves are roughly diamond-shaped or shaped like a goose's foot, with irregular teeth, and grow one at a time up the stem.
- Stems are upright, ridged, often with green or reddish stripes.
- Flowers are tiny, green and mealy, in dense clusters at the top and in the leaf joints. No coloured petals.
- No strong smell.
- Height 20 cm to 1.5 m.
- Grows in rich, disturbed soil: vegetable gardens, field edges, compost heaps, building sites, manure heaps.
- Young plants from April; flowers and seeds from July to October.

## lookalikes
Black nightshade (Solanum nigrum) is poisonous and grows in the same gardens and fields. Its leaves are dark green, often tinged purple, and have no white mealy powder. It has small white star-shaped flowers with a yellow cone in the middle, and green then black berries.

Thorn apple (Datura stramonium) is very poisonous and grows on the same disturbed ground. Its leaves are larger, dark green, with big pointed lobes, no mealy powder, and an unpleasant smell when crushed. It has large white trumpet flowers and spiny seed capsules.

Other goosefoots (Chenopodium) and oraches (Atriplex) look similar. Most are edible, but some smell strongly of fish or are less pleasant.

The white mealy powder on young leaves is the key feature. If it is missing, do not eat the plant.

## eat
- Young leaves and tender tips, cooked like spinach: boiled, steamed, in soups or with eggs.
- Boil it and discard the water to reduce oxalic acid and nitrate.
- A few young raw leaves in a salad are fine, but do not eat it raw in large amounts.
- Seeds can be collected in autumn, rinsed well to remove bitter saponins, and cooked like porridge or added to bread.
- Eat it as a normal portion of vegetables, not every day in large amounts. Avoid it if you have kidney stones or kidney problems.

## harvest
- Leaves and tips from May to August, before flowering. Pinching out the tips makes the plant branch and grow more.
- Seeds from September to October, when they are dark and dry.
- Avoid plants on heavily manured ground, near livestock, on sprayed fields or roadsides.
