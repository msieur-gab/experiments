The sticky plant that clings to clothes and dogs' fur. Young shoots are edible cooked, and its seeds can be roasted as a coffee substitute. It grows in hedges, woodland edges, gardens and nettle patches.

## identify
- The whole plant sticks to clothes because of tiny hooked bristles on stems, leaves and seeds.
- Narrow leaves grow in rings (whorls) of six to eight around the stem, like the spokes of a wheel.
- Stem is square, weak and scrambles over other plants, up to 1.5 m or more.
- Tiny white flowers with four petals, from May to September.
- Seeds are small, round, paired bristly balls that stick to fur and socks.
- No strong smell.
- Grows in hedges, woodland edges, gardens, field edges and among nettles, in rich soil.

## lookalikes
Other bedstraws (Galium) also have leaves in rings, but are not sticky. They are not dangerous, but are not the same plant.

Sweet woodruff (Galium odoratum) has leaves in rings, is not sticky, grows in woods, and smells of hay when dried. It contains coumarin and should only be used in small amounts.

No dangerous plant in Central Europe combines leaves in rings with a sticky, clinging stem.

## eat
- Young shoots, before flowering: chop and cook briefly like a green vegetable, or add to soups. Cooking softens the bristles.
- A handful of young shoots can be soaked in cold water overnight to make a fresh drink. Strain before drinking.
- Raw, the shoots are hairy and unpleasant to swallow except when very young and finely chopped.
- Ripe seeds can be roasted dark in a dry pan and ground as a coffee substitute. It contains no caffeine.
- Eat moderate amounts; it is a side green, not a staple.

## harvest
- Young shoots from February to May. They become tough and bristly once the plant flowers.
- Seeds from July to September, when they are brown and hard.
- Pick only the top tender part of each shoot. Wear gloves if your skin reacts.
- Do not pick from roadside hedges, sprayed field edges or dog-walking paths.
