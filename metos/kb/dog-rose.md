The dog rose is a common wild rose of hedges and woodland edges. Its red fruits, rose hips, stay on the bush well into winter and are rich in vitamin C, which makes them one of the few fresh wild foods available in the cold months.

## identify
- Fruit (hip) oval or egg-shaped, 1.5 to 2.5 cm, smooth, shiny orange-red to red.
- Inside: many hard, hairy seeds packed in a layer of flesh.
- Often with dried remains of the flower (a small crown or scar) at the tip.
- Arching, thorny stems up to about 3 m; thorns hooked and curved.
- Leaves of five to seven toothed oval leaflets.
- Simple flowers with five pink or white petals, in June.
- Grows in hedges, woodland edges, scrub and along field margins.

## lookalikes
All wild roses in Central Europe have edible hips and are prepared the same way.

Several poisonous shrubs carry red fruit in autumn: yew (a red cup open at the end with a dark seed), bryony (red berries on a climbing vine with tendrils), spindle (pink four-lobed capsules with orange seeds), guelder rose (shiny see-through red berries in flat clusters) and cotoneaster (small red berries, no thorns, simple leaves). None of these has a hip: an oval fruit full of hairy seeds, on a thorny plant with leaflets. Hawthorn has smaller dark red fruits in clusters with one or two stones; it is edible.

## eat
The flesh can be eaten raw after cutting the hip open and scraping out all seeds and hairs. More often hips are cooked: simmer, mash, then strain through a fine cloth to catch the hairs, and use as purée, syrup, jam or soup. Whole or halved dried hips make tea; strain through cloth. Frost or freezing makes hips softer and sweeter.

Petals are edible raw, in salad or syrup.

## harvest
Hips from about September to winter, when red and starting to soften; after the first frost they are easier to work. Petals in June. Wear gloves for the thorns. Avoid roadsides and sprayed field edges.
