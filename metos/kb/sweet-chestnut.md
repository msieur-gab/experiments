A large tree whose nuts are starchy and filling, and can be roasted, boiled, dried or ground into flour. It grows in warmer, lime-poor areas: much of France, southern Switzerland, the wine-growing parts of south-west Germany, and a few warm parts of Austria. It is also planted in parks.

## identify
- Husk green, turning yellow-brown, densely covered in long, thin, sharp spines, like a hedgehog. It hurts to hold.
- Usually two or three nuts per husk, glossy brown, flat on one side where they pressed together, ending in a pointed tip with a small pale tuft.
- Leaves long and narrow, about 15 to 25 cm, single (not in a hand-shaped group), with sharp, bristle-tipped teeth along the edge.
- Bark on older trees has deep ridges that often twist in a spiral up the trunk.
- Long creamy-yellow flower catkins in June and July, with a heavy smell.
- Nuts fall from late September to November.

## lookalikes
Horse chestnut (conker tree; Rosskastanie, marronnier d'Inde) is toxic and very common in towns, parks and avenues. Eating conkers causes vomiting, stomach pain and diarrhoea, and can be more serious.

- Leaves: horse chestnut has large hand-shaped leaves of five to seven leaflets spreading from one point. Sweet chestnut leaves are single.
- Husk: horse chestnut has a thick, fleshy husk with a few short, stubby spikes, or none. Sweet chestnut husks are covered all over in fine, dense spines.
- Nut: a conker is usually one per husk, large, round and shiny, with a big pale scar and no point or tuft. A sweet chestnut is flatter on one side and ends in a pointed tip with a tuft.
- Flowers: horse chestnut has upright white or pink "candles" in May.

In French, "marron" is used for both: sweet chestnuts are sold as marrons, while the horse chestnut tree is the marronnier d'Inde. Go by the features, not the name.

Beech nuts are much smaller and triangular. See beech.

## eat
- Always cook them. Raw sweet chestnuts are edible in small amounts but are astringent and hard to digest.
- Before roasting, cut a cross or a slit through the shell of each nut, or they can burst.
- Roast in embers, in a pan with holes, or in a hot oven, for roughly 20 to 30 minutes until the inside is soft. Peel while warm, removing the shell and the brown inner skin.
- Or boil scored nuts in water for about 20 to 30 minutes and peel.
- Dried, peeled chestnuts keep for months and can be ground into flour for flatbread, porridge or soup.
- They are mostly starch, so they can be eaten in meal-sized amounts like potatoes.

## harvest
- Late September to November. Collect nuts from the ground after they fall.
- Wear sturdy shoes and gloves. Opening a husk by pressing it with both boots works well.
- Discard nuts with small holes (insect larvae), mould, or a hollow rattle. Nuts that float in water are often bad.
- Fresh chestnuts spoil within days to weeks; dry or cook them to keep.
- Avoid trees along busy roads. Check the land rules: in some areas chestnut groves are private and gathering is restricted.
