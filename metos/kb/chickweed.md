title: Chickweed
aka: common chickweed, chickenweed, starweed, Vogelmiere, mouron des oiseaux, stellaire intermédiaire, Stellaria media
tags: plant wild food
warn: Do not confuse it with scarlet pimpernel, which is mildly poisonous and grows in the same places. Chickweed has white flowers and a single line of hairs along its stem.

A small, soft, sprawling plant that covers bare soil in gardens, fields and vegetable beds. It is mild, tender and can be eaten raw. It is one of the few wild greens you can find in most months, including mild winters.

## identify
- A single line of fine hairs runs along one side of the round stem, and switches side at each pair of leaves.
- Small white flowers with five petals, each split so deeply that they look like ten.
- Pull a stem gently apart: an elastic inner thread often holds the two ends together.
- Leaves in opposite pairs, oval and pointed, smooth, bright green; lower leaves have stalks, upper ones do not.
- No milky sap when broken.
- Low and sprawling, 5 to 40 cm long, often forming soft mats.
- Grows in rich, disturbed soil: gardens, vegetable beds, field edges, compost heaps.
- Can be found almost all year; it dies back in hot, dry summers.

## lookalikes
Scarlet pimpernel (Anagallis arvensis) grows in the same places and is mildly poisonous. Its flowers are orange-red, sometimes blue, never white. Its stem is square and hairless, and its leaves have small black dots underneath.

Spurges (Euphorbia), such as petty spurge, also grow in gardens and are poisonous. Broken spurge stems release white milky sap. Chickweed never does.

Mouse-ear chickweeds (Cerastium) look similar but are hairy all over and have a duller, grey-green look. They are not dangerous, but are less pleasant to eat.

If the flowers are not white, the stem has no single line of hairs, or the plant has milky sap, do not eat it.

## eat
- The leafy tips, stems and flowers can all be eaten raw in salads or sandwiches.
- It can also be added at the end of cooking to soups, eggs or potatoes, like spinach. Cook it only briefly.
- The taste is mild and fresh, a bit like young pea shoots or corn salad.
- It contains saponins. A normal salad portion is fine. Very large amounts may upset the stomach.

## harvest
- Best from March to May and from September to November, when it is lush.
- Cut the top few centimetres with scissors and leave the roots so it regrows. This also keeps soil out of the bunch.
- Wash well. Low-growing plants are easily soiled by animals.
- Do not pick it from roadsides, sprayed fields or flower beds, or where dogs are walked.
