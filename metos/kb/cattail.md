title: Cattail

A tall marsh plant with a brown, sausage-shaped head. Different parts are edible through the year, and the starchy roots can be a large source of food. It grows in shallow water at the edges of ponds, lakes, ditches and slow rivers, and in marshes.

## identify
- Seed head a firm, brown, velvety "sausage" on top of a stiff stem. Old heads stay standing through winter and burst into fluffy seed.
- Directly above the brown head sits a thinner spike of male flowers, which sheds yellow pollen in early summer and then withers to a bare tip.
- Leaves long, flat and strap-like, up to about 2 m, growing from the base in a flat fan, with no raised midrib.
- Cut across the leaf base: the inside is spongy, like foam, with a white tender core in young shoots.
- Roots thick, white, starchy rhizomes running through the mud, with pointed shoots.
- Grows in dense stands in water or wet mud.
- Pollen spikes June to July; brown heads from summer through winter.

## lookalikes
- Yellow flag iris (Sumpf-Schwertlilie, iris des marais) is toxic and grows in the same wet places. Its sword-shaped leaves are greyish-green, fan out flat and overlap at the base, and have a raised midrib. They are solid, not spongy inside. It has large yellow flowers in May and June and never has a brown sausage head. Its rhizome is pinkish to brown inside.
- Sweet flag (Kalmus, acore) looks similar but its leaves smell strongly sweet and spicy when crushed and have a midrib. Do not eat it.
- Bur-reed has strap-like leaves too, but its fruits are spiky green balls. Do not eat it.
- Water hemlock, one of the most poisonous plants in Europe, also grows in these places. See deadly-lookalikes.
- Iris and cattail can grow side by side. A brown head nearby does not prove that the shoot in your hand is cattail; check each one.

## eat
Cook all cattail parts, because of the water it grows in.
- Young shoots in spring: pull the shoot, peel away the outer leaves to the white, tender core at the base. Boil or fry it; it tastes a little like cucumber.
- Green male flower spike, before it turns yellow: boil a few minutes and nibble off the green flowers like corn on the cob.
- Pollen in early summer: shake into a bag and mix with flour for bread or pancakes.
- Rhizomes in autumn to early spring: wash, peel, cut and crush them in clean water. The starch settles at the bottom; pour off the water and fibres, and cook the starch into porridge or flatbread. Or roast pieces and chew out the starch.
- The core of shoots and rhizomes can be eaten in meal-sized amounts once cooked.

## harvest
- Shoots: April to June, when 30 to 60 cm tall.
- Green flower spikes: May to June. Pollen: June to July.
- Rhizomes: from late autumn to early spring, when they are full of starch. Wear waterproof boots and expect cold, muddy work.
- Do not pick in water near farmland run-off, roads, sewage outlets, industrial sites, or anywhere with oily film, foam or dead fish. The plant takes up pollution from the water.
- Wash all parts in clean water, then cook them.
