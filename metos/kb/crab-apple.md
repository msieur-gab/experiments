The crab apple is the wild apple of European woods and hedges, a small tree with small, hard, very sour apples. Wild-growing domestic apples are also common along paths and field edges. Both are useful cooked, keep well and are easy to recognise.

## identify
- Small tree or large shrub, up to about 10 m, often with a broad, irregular crown.
- Some twigs end in short spiny shoots on truly wild trees.
- Oval, finely toothed leaves, alternate, on short stalks.
- Pink-white flowers with five petals in April and May, clearly apple blossom.
- Fruit is a small apple, about 2 to 4 cm, yellow-green, sometimes flushed red, on a longish stalk.
- Cut across, it shows an apple core with a star of seed chambers and brown pips.
- Very sour and astringent raw.
- Grows in woods, woodland edges, hedges and scrub; escaped apple trees along roads and paths.

## lookalikes
Feral domestic apples have larger, sweeter fruit and are edible. Ornamental crab apples in parks and gardens have small apples that are also edible, though often very sour.

Wild pear has pear-shaped or round small fruit with gritty flesh; also edible, usually after storage. Quince is larger, yellow and furry, and is eaten cooked.

The name thorn apple (jimsonweed) belongs to a very poisonous, non-woody plant with large white trumpet flowers and a spiny, walnut-sized capsule full of black seeds. It is not an apple. Only pick apples from a tree.

## eat
Raw crab apples are edible but very sour; a few are fine. Cook them: they make clear jelly, sauce, and are rich in pectin, so they help other fruit set as jam. Cut, cook and strain through cloth to leave seeds behind. Slices can be dried. Juice can be made into vinegar.

Eating a few swallowed whole pips is not considered dangerous, but do not chew or grind them in quantity.

## harvest
From about September to November, when the fruit falls easily or after the first frost. Stored in a cool place, they soften and become less sour over a few weeks. Avoid fruit from busy roadsides. Check windfalls for rot and insects.
