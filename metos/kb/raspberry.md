The wild raspberry is the same plant as the garden raspberry, with smaller and often sweeter fruit. It grows in clearings, woodland edges and mountain slopes across Central Europe and is one of the easiest wild fruits to recognise.

## identify
- Red fruit made of many small juicy segments, slightly soft and hairy.
- The ripe fruit pulls off easily and leaves a white cone on the plant, so the fruit is hollow like a thimble.
- Upright, mostly unbranched canes up to about 2 m, with many fine, soft prickles rather than hooked thorns.
- Leaves split into three to seven toothed leaflets; the underside is clearly white or silvery.
- Small white flowers that hang down, in early summer.
- Grows in forest clearings, cut or burnt areas, woodland edges, often in large patches.

## lookalikes
Unripe blackberries are red too, but they are hard, sour and do not come off their core. Wait until they turn black.

Several poisonous shrubs in Central Europe have red berries: fly honeysuckle (red berries in pairs), mezereon (red berries stuck directly to the stem), guelder rose (shiny, see-through red berries in flat clusters), yew (a red cup around a dark seed), bryony (red berries on a climbing vine) and spindle (pink four-lobed capsules with orange seeds). None of them has a fruit made of packed beads that is hollow inside. If it is not hollow, do not eat it.

## eat
Ripe fruit is good raw, or as jam, syrup, sauce or dried fruit. Young leaves are dried for tea. Wash fruit, especially from low canes.

In parts of Central Europe the fox tapeworm is present, and some people cook or wash low-growing wild berries for that reason. The risk from berries is thought to be low but not zero; washing reduces it, cooking removes it, normal freezing does not.

## harvest
From about July to August, later in the mountains. Pick when deep red and the fruit slips off easily. Pick into a flat container, as the fruit crushes easily. Avoid roadsides and sprayed field edges.
