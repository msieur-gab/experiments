A many-stemmed shrub or small tree whose nuts are rich in fat and protein and keep for months in the shell. It is common in hedges, woodland edges, clearings and old coppice across Central Europe, from lowlands into the mountains.

## identify
- Nuts in clusters of one to four, each sitting in a green, leafy, ragged husk that is open at the tip and about as long as the nut.
- Ripe nuts are light brown with a hard, smooth shell and a pale rough patch at the base.
- Leaves almost round with a short pointed tip, double-toothed edges and a soft, slightly hairy surface; they alternate along the twig.
- Usually many straight stems from one base, rather than a single trunk.
- Bark smooth, grey-brown to coppery, often with small pale horizontal marks; thin outer layer may peel.
- Long yellow catkins ("lamb's tails") hang from bare twigs in late winter, January to March, before the leaves.
- Nuts ripen August to October.

## lookalikes
- Hornbeam has similar double-toothed leaves, but they are longer, with neat parallel pleats, and its fruit is a small nutlet on a three-lobed leafy wing. No real nut.
- Alder has roundish leaves too, but its fruit are small woody cones, and it grows by water.
- Turkish hazel, planted along streets and in parks, is a single-trunked tree with nuts in a fringed, bristly husk. Its nuts are edible but small and thick-shelled.
- Beech nuts are triangular and sit in a bristly woody husk. See beech.
- No common toxic plant in Central Europe bears a nut like hazel. The risk lies in spoiled nuts rather than lookalikes.

## eat
- Kernels raw or roasted. Roasting in a pan or by the fire for a few minutes improves flavour and loosens the brown skin.
- Unripe green nuts in August can be eaten fresh; they are soft and milky but do not keep.
- Crack the shell with a stone or nutcracker. Discard any kernel that is shrivelled, dark, mouldy or bitter.
- Ground nuts can be added to porridge, bread or flour.
- They are filling: a handful is a good snack. Anyone with a tree nut allergy should not eat them.

## harvest
- August to October. Ripe nuts loosen from the husk, turn brown and fall; shake the branches over a cloth.
- Squirrels, mice and birds take many of them, so early picking of nuts that are just turning brown is common.
- Nuts with a small round hole have been eaten by weevil larvae. Nuts that float in water are often empty or spoiled.
- Dry nuts in the shell in a cool, airy place, and check them for mould.
- Avoid hedges next to busy roads and sprayed fields.
