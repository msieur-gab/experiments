title: Chicory
aka: wild chicory, common chicory, blue sailors, succory, Wegwarte, Gemeine Wegwarte, Zichorie, chicorée sauvage, chicorée amère, Cichorium intybus

A tough plant with sky-blue flowers on stiff, almost leafless stems. Its bitter leaves are edible, the flowers can go in salad, and the roasted root is a classic coffee substitute. It grows on dry, sunny ground: field edges, waysides, waste ground and dry meadows, mostly in lowlands.

## identify
- Flowers clear sky-blue (rarely white or pink), about 3 to 4 cm, each petal square-ended with small teeth. They sit directly on the stem, with almost no stalk.
- Flowers open in the morning and usually close by early afternoon.
- Stems stiff, branched, grooved, 30 cm to over 1 m tall, with only small leaves clasping them.
- Basal leaves form a rosette and look like dandelion leaves: lobed, with teeth pointing backwards, often slightly hairy on the underside of the midrib.
- Broken leaves, stems and roots release white milky sap.
- Long, thick taproot.
- Flowers July to October.

## lookalikes
- Dandelion rosettes look almost identical before flowering. Both are edible; dandelion has one yellow flower per hollow stem. See dandelion.
- Ragwort (Jakobskreuzkraut, séneçon jacobée) is toxic to the liver and also forms rosettes of deeply lobed leaves on dry grassland. It has no milky sap, often has a purplish leaf base and smells unpleasant when crushed, and later has clusters of yellow daisy-like flowers.
- Foxglove rosettes have soft, felty, unlobed leaves and no milky sap. They are toxic to the heart. See deadly-lookalikes.
- Cornflower is also blue, but its flowers are ragged, fringed trumpets on a softer, greyish plant. Its flowers are edible too, but do not mistake one for the other when eating leaves.
- In flower, chicory is hard to confuse: blue, square-tipped petals stuck directly on bare, stiff stems.

## eat
- Young leaves from the rosette, raw in salad. They are bitter; mix them with milder greens.
- Older leaves: boil them, pour away the water, and boil again in fresh water to reduce the bitterness.
- Flowers raw in salad, picked in the morning while open.
- Roots: scrub, chop, dry and roast until dark brown, then grind for a coffee-like drink. Young roots can also be boiled as a vegetable.
- Eat like other bitter greens: a side dish rather than the whole meal. Large amounts can upset the stomach.

## harvest
- Rosette leaves: March to May, and again in autumn when new rosettes form.
- Flowers: July to October, in the morning.
- Roots: autumn of the first year or early spring, before the flowering stem grows.
- Its favourite places are roadsides, car parks and field edges, which are often polluted or sprayed. Look for plants on paths and meadows well away from traffic and crops.
