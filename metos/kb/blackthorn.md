Blackthorn is a dense, very thorny shrub of hedges and woodland edges, covered in white blossom in early spring before its leaves appear. Its small blue-black fruits, sloes, are too sour to eat raw in quantity but are useful cooked, and they stay on the bush late into autumn.

## identify
- Dense, spreading shrub, usually 1 to 4 m, often forming thickets.
- Dark, almost black bark; side shoots end in long, stiff, sharp thorns.
- Pure white five-petalled flowers in March and April, before the leaves.
- Small oval leaves, finely toothed, alternate.
- Round fruit about 1 to 1.5 cm, blue-black with a pale blue waxy bloom that rubs off.
- Each fruit holds one hard stone, like a small plum.
- Very sour and mouth-drying taste.
- Grows in hedges, field margins, scrub and woodland edges.

## lookalikes
Bullace and wild plums are close relatives with larger fruit; they are edible and prepared the same way.

Buckthorn has black berries, but they are shiny without a waxy bloom, contain several small seeds rather than one stone, and its leaves are rounder with veins curving towards the tip. Its berries are a strong laxative and should not be eaten. Privet and dogwood have black berries in clusters, without a stone. Deadly nightshade is a soft, non-woody plant with shiny black berries on a green star; it is very poisonous.

A sloe is a small blue plum: a waxy blue-black skin and one stone. If there is no single stone, it is not a sloe.

## eat
Raw sloes are very astringent; a few are harmless if the stones are spat out, but they are not pleasant. Frost or a night in the freezer softens them and reduces the sourness. Cook into jam, jelly, syrup, juice or sauce, usually mixed with sweeter fruit; strain out the stones after cooking. Do not crush the stones while cooking.

## harvest
From about October to December, ideally after the first frosts. Wear thick gloves: blackthorn punctures often become infected, so clean any wound. Avoid roadsides and sprayed field edges.
