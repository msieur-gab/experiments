title: Burdock
aka: greater burdock, lesser burdock, beggar's buttons, gobo, Große Klette, Kleine Klette, Klette, grande bardane, bardane, Arctium lappa, Arctium minus

A large plant known for its burrs that hook onto clothes and fur. Its long taproot is a filling cooked vegetable, eaten in Japan as gobo, and its young stems are edible too. It grows on rich, disturbed ground: field edges, farmyards, waste ground, woodland edges and paths.

## identify
- Burrs: round, about 1 to 4 cm, covered in hooked bristles that cling to clothing. Old brown burrs often stay on dead stems through winter.
- Leaves very large, broad and heart-shaped, wavy-edged, dull green on top and grey-white and woolly underneath.
- First year: only a low rosette of big leaves. Second year: a tall branched stem, up to about 2 m.
- Flowers purple, thistle-like, sitting on top of the burrs, July to September.
- Root a long, brown, carrot-shaped taproot, white inside, which can go deep.
- Leaf stalks often reddish at the base.

## lookalikes
- Butterbur (Pestwurz, pétasite) grows by streams and in damp places and has huge, roundish leaves with a deep notch at the stalk, grey underneath. It contains substances that damage the liver. It flowers in early spring as pink spikes before the leaves appear, and never makes hooked burrs.
- Foxglove rosettes have smaller, narrower, soft felty leaves, not broad and heart-shaped. They are toxic to the heart. See deadly-lookalikes.
- Rhubarb leaves are toxic. Rhubarb grows in gardens, has thick red or green stalks, and its leaves are glossy without a woolly underside.
- Coltsfoot has smaller, toothed, heart-shaped leaves with a felty underside, and yellow flowers in early spring.
- Plants in the burrs' neighbourhood are not proof: check each plant's leaves before digging. Roots alone are hard to identify.

## eat
- Root: scrub or peel it, slice it thinly, and boil, stew or stir-fry until tender, roughly 20 minutes or more. It is earthy and slightly sweet. Soaking slices in water first reduces bitterness and browning.
- Young flower stems in late spring, before the flower buds open: peel off the bitter outer skin and cook the inner part like asparagus or celery.
- Very young leaf stalks can be peeled and cooked. Leaves are very bitter and are rarely worth eating.
- Eat it cooked. A root or two makes a vegetable side dish; start with a small amount if you have not eaten it before.

## harvest
- Roots from first-year plants (rosettes with no tall stem) in autumn, September to November, or in early spring before the stem grows. Roots of flowering plants are woody.
- Dig deep with a spade or stick beside the root; it breaks easily.
- Flower stems: May to June, while still soft.
- Wear long sleeves in summer; the burrs stick to clothes and hair.
- Avoid field edges that may be sprayed and ground near roads or old dumps.
