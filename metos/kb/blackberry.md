Blackberries grow on bramble, a thorny, arching shrub found almost everywhere in Central Europe. The fruit is common, easy to recognise and safe once identified, which makes it one of the most useful wild foods of late summer.

## identify
- Fruit made of many small round juicy segments packed together, green then red then glossy black when ripe.
- When picked, the ripe fruit comes off with its white core still inside (unlike raspberry, which leaves the core on the plant).
- Long arching or trailing stems with hooked prickles that catch on clothes.
- Leaves split into three or five toothed leaflets, spread like a hand; often prickly along the central vein underneath.
- White or pale pink flowers with five petals, from early summer, often on the same plant as ripe fruit later on.
- Grows in hedges, woodland edges, clearings, waste ground and along paths.

## lookalikes
Dewberry is a smaller, low-growing relative whose fruit has fewer segments and a bluish waxy coating. It is also edible.

The real danger is mixing up blackberries with single black berries. Deadly nightshade has shiny, cherry-sized black berries sitting on a green five-pointed star, on a soft, non-woody plant without thorns. It is very poisonous. Buckthorn, privet, dogwood, ivy and elder also carry black berries, but each berry is single and smooth, often in clusters, and none of them is made of packed segments. If the fruit is not made of many small beads, do not eat it.

## eat
Ripe black fruit can be eaten raw, cooked into jam, sauce or compote, or dried. Red or hard fruit is unripe and sour. Wash fruit picked near the ground. Young leaves are sometimes dried for tea.

## harvest
From about late July to September, depending on altitude and weather. Pick only fully black, soft fruit; the best fruit is often at the tips. Avoid fruit at dog height along busy paths, near roads and on sprayed field edges. Late-season fruit that is mouldy, watery or full of insects is better left.
