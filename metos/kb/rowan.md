The rowan is a small, slender tree with large clusters of bright orange-red berries in late summer and autumn. It is common in woods, mountains and towns across Central Europe. The berries are bitter and mildly toxic raw but become a useful food when cooked.

## identify
- Small tree up to about 15 m, with smooth, shiny grey bark.
- Long leaves made of 9 to 19 narrow, toothed leaflets in pairs with one at the end, arranged alternately on the twig.
- Large flat clusters of small creamy-white flowers in May and June, with a strong smell.
- Large, dense clusters of round berries, about 1 cm, bright orange to scarlet, from August.
- A small five-pointed star at the end of each berry, opposite the stalk.
- Grows in woods, forest edges, mountains up to high altitude, hedges, parks and streets.

## lookalikes
Service tree and other Sorbus species (whitebeam, wild service tree) have larger fruit that is brown or red when ripe. Their leaves are different: whitebeam leaves are whole and white below. Their fruit is eaten soft and overripe.

Red-berried elder has red berries in egg-shaped clusters and leaves in opposite pairs with five to seven leaflets; its seeds are toxic. Guelder rose has shiny translucent red berries and lobed leaves; poisonous raw. Yew, bryony, spindle and holly also have red fruit in autumn, but none has long leaves of many leaflets with large flat clusters of orange berries. Hawthorn has dark red berries, lobed leaves and thorns.

Ash has similar leaves but no berries; it carries bunches of winged seeds and has black buds.

## eat
Always cook the berries. Remove stalks, simmer until soft and use for jelly, jam, syrup or sauce, often mixed with apples or other fruit. Cooking breaks down the substance (parasorbic acid) that irritates the stomach. Freezing or frost first makes them less bitter, but they still need cooking. Use moderate amounts. Strain to remove most seeds.

Some cultivated sweet varieties exist in gardens; wild trees should be treated as described here.

## harvest
From about August to October, when fully coloured; many people wait for the first frost or freeze them overnight before cooking. Cut whole clusters. Birds take them quickly. Avoid street trees and roadsides.
